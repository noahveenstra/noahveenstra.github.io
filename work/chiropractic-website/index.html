Creating a Responsive Website for a Local Chiropractor’s Office

Client work · 2022

TLDR; Over the summer, I was brought on to run the multimedia presence of a chiropractic office. My main goal was to identify user’s needs for a Chiropractic site through a comparative analysis and speaking with some users of the current site. By researching, I was able to tailor the experience of the new website for this little pocket of users in South East Michigan.

Background

Over the summer, I was brought on to run the multimedia presence of a chiropractic office. This entailed running their Facebook and Instagram account, and eventually, I was provided the opportunity to create a new site for them. This was the first time I was offered to create a public site for anyone, as well as a budget to manage.

I believe there are many ways to quantify the process of design and development, as it’s very fluid and there often times is not any structure to it. For this study, I’m using Ryan Ford’s Design Odyssey Framework, as I believe it aligns with my process more than others.

What are we trying to do and why?

To begin the process of creating a whole new identity online, I had to start asking big questions. One of them being what am I even trying to achieve and why? What is the meaning behind the site, and how does it serve the end user?

Research

I started by conducting a comparative analysis of parallel competitors. I focused mainly on some of the highest rated chiropractic offices in different areas of the midwest. To achieve this, I used a combination of Yelp and HealthGrades to search for the offices that see a high volume of patients and are still highly rated. From these two sites, I was able to pick 5 chiropractic offices that featured modern sites I could draw from. I also made sure to look at the current site…

Not too much to behold… It was built using GoDaddy, so whoever built this first site was obviously limited by their tools. But, there were some elements that I was able to take from this site, surprisingly.

Findings

My main goal was to identify the main things that all these sites shared. Since they were all within the same vein, I found there were numerous similarities. Most started with a welcoming message stating the name of the practice, followed by a couple of prominent procedures or unique features of the practice. This is where I usually found the differentiators. Another prominent and consistent element was a live and accurate section for the current hours. This element would be a dynamic and changing element every day to make sure it was up-to-date. And lastly, I found that there was almost always a hero section. It typically would contain pictures of the practice’s building, a variety of the team working there, and some interior pictures.

User Needs and Empathy

After my preliminary research was done, I took some time to think about what a typical patient would want to see from a chiropractor’s site.

Personas

John

Starting with a persona of John. A 46-year-old male whose lifestyle is sedimentary behind a desk. He is a father of three and lives with his wife. They’ve just become empty nesters. John used to be active in his early teens and twenties, but as he got older, his priorities shifted to his family and work. Recently, he started feeling pain in his back and is beginning to seek a local chiropractor. John thinks that most technology is a pain and flimsy overall. He’d much rather book an appointment in person or on the phone instead of an online portal.

Stacy

Stacy is a 32-year-old single female who is fairly active throughout the week, exercising at least 3 times. At work, she’s also fairly sedimentary, although her office just bought new standing/sitting desks but meetings in conference rooms that occupy most of her time continue to be sitting. The favorite part of her day is getting home to be greeted by her dog Mabel. A golden lab who takes care of herself but is always happy to have the company of Stacy to go outside and throw the toy around for her. She prioritizes health and makes sure to get a yearly checkup. She enjoys being able to sign up for her appointments online, adding as much detail as she thinks necessary and on her time. There’s also a small bit of anxiety that’s been associated with the phone call method.

Plan

After sitting with these findings and personas for a couple of days, I synthesized a plan I believed would take into consideration most, if not all, of these factors. First, make the site mobile friendly. It’s widely known that most of all the internet traffic on the planet is from mobile devices. Second, avoid creating a full scheduling application on the website. If I had sunk money into developing this extension, I do believe it would increase the UX of the site as there would be more options available for the users. Third, make it effortless to contact the practice. One thing that all websites and the personas had in common was the necessity to contact. And the easier I can make it, the more straightforward, the better the user will be able to use the site for what it’s truly meant for.

Explore and Play

This stage of the design and development process is arguably the most fun. It’s the stage that I enjoy messing up in, and I can’t say that about many things. This is where the most creative aspects of a project can shine, and I get to flex those muscles. It’s also where I see the most improvement in my designs because of review and critiquing.

Wireframes

It’s good practice to begin by creating a rough wireframe of the site before we even touch the computer.

Once I had a rough idea and got some feedback from the client, I jumped right into high fidelity wire framing. This has to be my favorite part of the process for me. Many times things are reordered or redesigned completely in this stage. I believe that’s because once you start adding colors and animations and seeing what the site is really going to look and feel like, there is much room for change and inspiration to run freely.

I made sure to include some of the most prominent elements from the original website and tried my best to make it feel sleek and modern on all types of screens. Furthermore, I wanted to produce a well-thought-out, modern site for my client.

Flow chart

Another important piece of this stage is mapping out a user’s flow for multiple cases. I wanted to make sure that information was not buried, and the user was able to find it within 3 clicks. With these goals, I made this very abbreviated flow chart.

Prototyping

After I was finished thinking and planning about how to create a new site effectively for the proper audience, I create a prototype. I hand coded a couple of the pages into a test site on my current portfolio website that the client would be able to go to with a special address and password. There, they were able to navigate around and get a sense of the new look and feel I was going for. With some great feedback, both positive and negative, I was capable of making changes for something they’d be proud of and really enjoy.

Build and Play

Finally, we were ready to start building out all the pages and optimizing for multiple screen sizes. Construction always seems so fun in the beginning when you start laying down the main components. It’s really only when you try to achieve the SUPER COOL HOVER EFFECT or a REALLY GREAT CAROUSEL OF IMAGES that wrinkles start coming into play. “Well, I didn’t want the hover effect to be activated here because the contrast is thrown off.” “But the carousel needs to have absolute positioning, so everything needs to be reworked.” These are the types of hurdles that were constantly cropping up during this process. That, coupled with a lot of back and forth between the client, made this stage by far the most challenging, but also more rewarding. Planning and making a beautiful PDF or PNG is an entirely different feeling from creating a fluid, active, site. This kind of distinction is something I care about deeply and where I believe my hard and soft skills are used best. Because I was put in this “in-between” position, I was also able to make design decisions on the fly. If something wasn’t working or if something worked really well, I adjusted the design to the website then and there. I didn’t need to wait for developers to code my wireframe and me telling them that I needed to make a big change, I was able to do it. Finally, after a couple of weeks, we could publish the brand-new site.

Conclusion

I think the most prominent thing I learned from this project was creating not just for myself, but for another brand, and of course, for the other users. I think this is contrasted from the site you’re probably reading this on. My portfolio site was created to showcase my skills and abilities rather than the features, acclaims, and skills by a whole other business.
