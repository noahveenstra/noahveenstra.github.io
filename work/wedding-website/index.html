The first impression matters

I designed a custom wedding experience for two groups with different needs: guests who need a personal, straightforward way to respond, and the two of us who need to update information and manage the details. I used editable prefilled information, household invitation codes, and in-context editing to address those needs. An early guest appreciated the story section, and guests appreciated prefilled information; the RSVP flow still awaits real-world use.

When Leah and I started planning our wedding, I wanted our website to communicate the same care we were putting into the day itself. Many wedding sites felt interchangeable to me. A custom experience gave us room to make that first interaction feel personal.

I designed the direction and provided wireframes, goals, and detailed feedback; AI generated the code. The site evolved from an initial save-the-date experience into a redesigned website with an RSVP flow, content management, and a Stripe-based donation experience.

A navigation that feels alive

The earliest navigation was a basic row of links. I wanted it to feel playful, fluid, and polished while still making the site easy to explore.

I directed it toward a floating, sticky pill with a blurred background, adaptive text contrast, and a moving indicator for the active and hovered links. Dropdowns animate and transition between sections to create a sense of continuity. The exact easing and contrast took many rounds of review: I needed to experience them in context before they felt right.

In informal feedback, a coworker noticed the navigation and said the time spent refining it was worthwhile. Coworkers who explored the site did not raise navigation problems; this was not a formal usability study.

Less work for guests

I wanted the RSVP experience to collect what we needed without asking guests to do unnecessary work. The flow is tailored to our wedding and guides each household through its responses. It omits meal selection for our buffet reception.

Where Leah and I already had information, the save-the-date experience could prefill it while leaving it editable. Guests told us they appreciated that. Household invitation codes were chosen to limit casual access to a household’s information; they are a layer of protection, not a security guarantee.

The RSVP flow is ready for use, but invitations have not yet been sent, so its usability and completion outcomes remain to be tested with guests.

Giving both of us control

Updating content through GitHub felt impractical, and a separate admin portal made it difficult to tell which part of the page we were editing. I wanted Leah to be able to make changes herself, in context.

I directed a site-based editing experience for signed-in admins: selecting content opens an editor for that piece of the page. Leah tried it and said it was a major improvement over the admin portal. It isn’t direct typing on the page, and the editor can obscure some context, so there is still room to improve it.

A process of intentional iteration

The first save-the-date version took about a month. I reviewed and refined each section through many iterations, using mockups and specific goals to guide the AI toward the experience I envisioned. The current site is a full redesign for the RSVP phase.

Next, I want to observe guests using the RSVP flow and use what we learn to make it clearer. I also want to improve the editing experience so changes remain visible in context.
