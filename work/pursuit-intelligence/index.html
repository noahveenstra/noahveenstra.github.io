Case study · Local proof of concept

Pursuit Intelligence

Finding the experience behind the claim, then checking the source before it goes into a proposal.

My role

Originated the problem and concept; set product direction and information architecture; directed AI assisted development; reviewed retrieval quality and iterated on the workflow.

Scope and status

Local evidence library used on proposal work. Its learning is informing a national version now in development. The national system has not rolled out.

“I know we’ve done something like this. Where was it?” That question is familiar in proposal work. A team may have the experience, but the story is buried in years of past proposals, fact sheets, résumés, and response documents. Finding it can depend on remembering a project name or knowing which colleague to call.

I started Pursuit Intelligence to make that search less dependent on memory. The point was never to let AI write a claim and have the team accept it. I wanted a research workflow that could surface a promising experience, show where it came from, and leave the decision to the person responsible for the proposal.

Public note: Client, project, and source identifiers are omitted. The local proof of concept has informed national development; that version is still in development. No aggregate time savings, adoption rate, or proposal win impact has been measured.

The hard part was remembering the right evidence

A construction proposal is a response to a particular owner, project, delivery method, and set of evaluation criteria. Generic capability language can establish that a firm does the work. A specific project story can show how the team handled a constraint, coordinated people in the field, worked with the owner and architect, or kept an occupied campus operating.

Those examples do not live in one tidy library. The details are distributed across documents created for different pursuits and organized for the needs of the proposal that originally used them. A project résumé row might tell you that a person worked on a school. A fact sheet might list the square footage and delivery method. An old response might contain a paragraph about phasing construction around students. Those pieces are related, but they are not interchangeable.

When a new pursuit asks for an unusually specific example, keyword search can miss it because the old document used different language. A broad search can also return a project name, biography, or scope description that looks relevant but does not answer the question. The user has to do two jobs at once: find a lead and decide whether it actually supports the claim.

The opportunity I saw was to move the effort from “which file or person might remember this?” toward “what happened, where is the evidence, and does it fit this pursuit?”

A project record is not the same thing as a story

The most important information architecture decision was to define what a useful result means. A project name, team résumé, square footage, or list of services can be a helpful lead. None of those, on its own, is a reusable story.

A project story is a grounded account of an experience: what constraint or need arose, what the team did, how it coordinated with the owner, architect, superintendent, project manager, and trades, and what the experience may demonstrate for a new pursuit. The relevant people, business unit, market, delivery method, and source document provide context around that account. They help a proposal writer judge whether a story from one job truly maps to another.

This distinction matters because a system can return a technically related fact and still fail the user. If a query asks how a team handled work on an occupied campus, “renovated a 90,000 square foot school” does not answer it. The useful evidence would describe phasing, communication, or another action taken to keep the school operating. The record needs to help the user move from a project that might fit to the specific experience that proves why it fits.

A lead, not yet a story

Project facts

Building type, size, scope, delivery method, team résumé, or a list of services. These can narrow the search, but they do not prove what happened.

A candidate for reuse

An experience with evidence

A specific constraint, action, collaboration, and result, connected to a source a person can reopen and evaluate.

There is a further distinction between a story that exists and an authorized story. A large project portfolio may contain thousands of project records while only a smaller set of narratives has been reviewed and cleared for reuse. Treating every mention as ready to publish would erase an important review boundary. The system should help people find candidates; the source and the organization's review process determine what is safe to reuse.

A research workflow with a person in the loop

The workflow is deliberately a sequence of research decisions, not a one click answer. A search result is a starting point. It has to survive a source check and a fit check before its language is adapted for a live pursuit.

  1. Translate the pursuit question

    Start with the actual need in the owner’s request: a type of experience, constraint, relationship, delivery approach, or project context. Preserve the important qualifiers instead of searching only for a broad building type.

  2. Search for candidate experiences

    Look across prior proposal material for accounts that may answer the need, even when the older document describes the work in different words. A candidate is a lead to investigate, not an approved claim.

  3. Read the result in context

    Understand what the source was answering and what surrounding details qualify the passage. A résumé listing or project description may help identify a job without showing that the team performed the specific work being requested.

  4. Open the original evidence

    Compare the proposed wording with the original source page. Check the language, context, and limitations before carrying anything forward.

  5. Make the reuse decision

    The proposal writer decides whether the experience fits the new pursuit, what it supports, and how to express it accurately. If the source does not substantiate the claim, the result should not be used as proof.

The local proof of concept supported this research loop. This description does not imply that every step is automated or that the national platform already implements the full model.

A small, specific search made the idea concrete

One pursuit needed to know whether the firm had hosted a career fair on a prior project with a particular community context. The example was specific enough that a general list of projects or a search for the word “school” would not answer it. The useful match had to connect an event, a project, and the context that made it relevant.

The local workflow surfaced a career fair account from a prior proposal and adapted its wording for the current pursuit. That was a useful lead, but the generated phrasing was not the finish line. I asked for a screenshot of the original source page so I could compare the summary against the passage it came from.

That check is the important part of the example. The system helped recover a story that was hard to find by memory; the source let me examine what was actually documented; and I kept responsibility for deciding whether the account fit the live pursuit. The work was discovery plus judgment, not a claim that the model independently validated the story.

The public version intentionally withholds the client, project, and source page details. This preserves the structure of the problem without exposing proposal material.

Trust came from making evidence inspectable

Proposal language can represent the firm on a multimillion dollar project. A claim that sounds plausible but is not supported can create reputational risk and put the pursuit team in a bad position during an interview. That made source visibility a product requirement, not an optional citation treatment.

I did not want an answer that merely sounded confident. I wanted to know which prior page it came from and to be able to compare the claim with that page. In the local workflow, I asked for screenshots of the exact source pages behind promising results. I could then check whether a summary preserved the original meaning or introduced something the source did not say.

The difference between “related” and “supported” became central:

Related

Worth opening

A project, person, or phrase resembles the new request. It is a candidate for review, not evidence by itself.

Supported

Worth considering for reuse

The source describes the relevant action in context, and a person judges that it fits the current question and is cleared for use.

This approach keeps the human role concrete. People do not just approve an AI answer in the abstract. They inspect a source, interpret its limits, and decide what claim it can carry.

Wrong matches showed what the system misunderstood

Early results sometimes surfaced project biographies, scope descriptions, or square footage as if they were project stories. These were not random errors. They exposed a category problem: the system had found information associated with a project, but had not found an account of an experience that answered the question.

What I saw

A fact mistaken for proof

A project résumé, scope summary, or building size appeared in place of the real event or action the pursuit asked about.

What I changed

A sharper test for relevance

I compared the match with its source, explained why it missed, and refined the distinction between a project fact and an experience that actually happened.

I treated each wrong match as feedback about the product’s definition of relevance. I reviewed what the system returned, asked where it came from, and pointed out the gap between the query and the evidence. That repeated review helped sharpen what the workflow should surface and what a user still needed to verify.

The hardest tradeoff was not simply making search broader. Broader retrieval can expose more possible leads, but it can also make a person sort through more noise. The goal was to find material with enough depth and specificity to be useful while preserving a clear path back to the source.

Why I started with a local evidence library

The source material can include proposal responses, project details, and information about the people who worked on jobs. I built the proof of concept around a private local corpus on disk rather than treating sensitive material as ordinary content for a cloud search product. That was a practical trust boundary while exploring how the workflow should behave.

Local first also kept the experiment appropriately scoped. It let me test the core question on real proposal work: could an AI assisted workflow help surface a specific story, and could I inspect enough of its evidence to decide whether it belonged in a response? It did not turn the prototype into a shared team platform, and I do not describe it as one.

There is a meaningful product boundary here. A local research tool can help one person find and examine material. A national system has to support broader information flow, ownership, review, and reuse across the organization. Lessons from the first workflow can inform that design, but they do not mean the larger system is already deployed.

The work now informs a national effort

I initiated the project and directed its development: the original problem, research workflow, definition of a useful story, information architecture, evidence expectations, and iterative review were mine. I used AI to help implement the local proof of concept, then evaluated its output against the needs of actual proposal work.

I used the local system on real pursuits, and colleagues came to me for hard to find stories and data. It remained a local workflow, which made its limits visible as well as its value. After learning about the tool and seeing its ability to find specific stories in prior proposals, IT began developing a national version.

I continue to contribute weekly to architecture and information flow discussions, answer pipeline questions, and share outputs from the local system. That work connects the practical questions I encountered, such as what qualifies as a story and how someone checks a source, to decisions being made for the broader tool.

The next challenges are making results easier to use and improving their speed, depth, and specificity. The national version is still in development, so I do not claim measured organization wide adoption or time savings. The outcome so far is a working local research loop and a direct line from that learning into national product development.

The principle I keep returning to

Make hard to find experience easier to discover. Make the evidence easy to inspect. Keep the judgment with the person using it.

let’s build something awesome.
