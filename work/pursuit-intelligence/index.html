Case study · Local proof of concept

Pursuit Intelligence

Helping people find the story without giving up their judgment

Role

Concept, product direction, information architecture, testing, and AI assisted development

Status

Local proof of concept
National version in development

What if the answer to “I think we’ve done something like this” didn’t depend on remembering which proposal it lived in?

I initiated Pursuit Intelligence to help my construction marketing team find specific project experiences buried in years of past proposals. I directed an AI assisted build of a local research workflow that finds relevant material, shows its source, and leaves the final judgment to the person using it. I shaped the experience around a specific tension: reduce the effort of finding evidence without asking someone to trust an answer they cannot check.

Public note: Examples omit client, project, and source identifiers. The local proof of concept has informed national development; the national version is not yet rolled out. No aggregate time savings or adoption metrics have been measured.

The problem

When a team proposes a firm for a construction project, a specific account of what happened on a past job can be more useful than a general list of capabilities. But those stories were scattered across years of proposals.

During one pursuit, we needed a particularly specific example. I knew the information was likely already there, but finding it meant combing through old documents or asking colleagues who might remember it. I wanted to take that search and recall burden off the team without taking away their discernment.

The approach

I created and directed the development of a local evidence library, using AI to help implement it. I owned the original idea, the workflow, the information architecture, the decisions about evidence, and the iterative review of its output.

The goal was to offload the work of remembering where information lives, while keeping a person responsible for interpreting and applying it.

Finding a story that fit

For one pursuit, we needed to know whether the firm had hosted a career fair on a previous project with a particular community context. Relevant projects were a small part of the portfolio, making it a difficult story to locate by memory.

After a few minutes, the system surfaced a relevant career-fair story from a prior proposal and adapted its wording for the current pursuit. I then asked for a screenshot of the original source page so I could compare the proposed wording with what the source actually said.

Designing for trust

Proposals can concern multimillion-dollar projects. An unsupported claim could put a pursuit and the firm’s reputation at risk. Even though I was building the system, I wasn’t ready to trust its answers without checking them.

I began asking for screenshots of the exact source pages behind its answers. That gave me a concrete comparison: the proposed wording beside the original passage, with a person deciding what the source supported.

Teaching the system what a story is

Early results sometimes labeled project biographies, scope descriptions, or square footage as project stories. Those details could be useful, but they weren’t what I was looking for: an account of something that actually happened on a job.

Early output

A project fact

Biographies, scope descriptions, and square footage were being returned as project stories.

My decision

An experience with evidence

I reviewed wrong matches against their sources and refined the distinction: a useful story describes something that actually happened on a job.

I reviewed the output, asked where it came from, and explained why each wrong match missed the mark. I repeated that process to refine the workflow’s distinction between a project fact and an actual experience. Ease of use and trustworthy retrieval remained the hardest things to get right.

From a local tool to a national effort

I used the local system on real proposal work, and colleagues came to me for hard to find stories and data. It remained a local workflow rather than a shared platform.

After learning about the tool and seeing its ability to find specific stories in prior proposals, IT began developing a national version. I continue to help weekly, contributing to architecture and information-flow discussions, answering pipeline questions, and sharing outputs from my system. Those conversations connect the needs I encountered in proposal work with the decisions IT is making about how the national tool will work.

My next priority would be making the output easier to use and improving the speed, depth, and specificity of results. The central principle stays the same: make information easier to find, and make the evidence easier for people to judge.
